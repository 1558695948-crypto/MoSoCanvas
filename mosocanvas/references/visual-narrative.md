# Visual narrative

## Give each frame a verb

Name the frame by what changes: `invites`, `waits`, `records`, `anticipates`, `withholds`,
`mirrors`, `reverses`, or `leaves`. Nouns describe inventory; verbs define narrative pressure.

For a single image, encode a before and after through evidence:

- an interrupted action;
- an object already responding;
- a reflection that arrives early;
- traces whose cause is outside the frame;
- a spatial relationship about to change.

## Control information

Define three layers:

1. what the viewer sees immediately;
2. what becomes visible after scanning;
3. what remains unresolved.

Do not explain the third layer with redundant symbols or text. Ambiguity is useful only when the
first two layers are controlled.

## Plan a series as an argument

Use beats such as:

1. establish an ordinary rule;
2. introduce an anomalous response;
3. reveal that the response precedes the human request;
4. widen the system beyond one person;
5. create intimacy or complicity;
6. reverse observer and observed;
7. leave a quiet unresolved consequence.

The exact arc may change, but each frame must add information or alter power. Remove a frame if the
series means the same thing without it.

## Separate recurrence from repetition

Recurring elements may include a subject grammar, aperture, color accent, spatial interval, or
material behavior. Do not repeat the same camera distance, pose, centered silhouette, or lighting
effect simply to force consistency.

## Review without the prompt

Show the images in order with only frame numbers. Ask:

- What changed between adjacent frames?
- Who appears to know more?
- Where does attention return to the viewer?
- Which frame is redundant?
- Is the final state earned by earlier evidence?

If the arc requires reading the prompt, the visual narrative is not yet carrying the concept.
